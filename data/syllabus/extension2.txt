## 12.1 The nature of proof
### 12.1.1 The language and notation of proof
12.1.1.1 Use the formal language of proof, including the terms 'statement', 'proposition', 'implication', 'converse', 'negation', 'contradiction', 'counterexample', 'equivalence' and 'contrapositive'
12.1.1.2 Define a statement or proposition as a sentence that is either true or false, but not both
12.1.1.3 Use the notation $P \land Q$ to represent the statement '$P\ \text{and }Q$' and the notation $P \vee Q$ to represent the statement '$P\text{ or }Q$'
12.1.1.4 Define and use the negation of $P$ as 'not $P$', denoted $\neg P$ or $\sim P$
12.1.1.5 Define an implication as an 'if–then' statement, where 'if $P$ then $Q$' is denoted $P \Rightarrow Q$ or $P \rightarrow Q$, read as '$P$ implies $Q$'
12.1.1.6 Use the quantifiers 'for all' $(\forall)$, and 'there exists' $(\exists)$ in formulating statements
12.1.1.7 Negate statements including the negation of a negation $\neg(\neg P) = P$, the negation of an implication $\neg(P \Rightarrow Q) = \left( P\ \text{and ¬}Q \right) = \left( P\ \land \text{ ¬}Q \right)$, the negation $\neg(P\ and\ Q) = (\neg P\ or\ \neg Q)$, that is $\neg(P\ \land \ Q) = (\neg P\ \vee \ \neg Q)$, and the negation $\neg\left( P\ \text{or} Q \right) = (\neg P\ and\ \neg Q)$, that is $\neg\left( P\ \vee Q \right) = (\neg P\ \land \ \neg Q)$, noting that $P \Rightarrow Q = \neg\left( P\ \text{and ¬}Q \right) = (\neg P\ \text{or }Q)$, that is $P \Rightarrow Q = \neg\left( P\ \land \text{ ¬}Q \right) = (\neg P\ \vee Q)$
12.1.1.8 Define and use the converse of 'if $P$ then $Q$' as 'if $Q$ then $P$', denoted $Q \Rightarrow P$
12.1.1.9 Recognise that the converse of a true implication may or may not be true
12.1.1.10 Define equivalence of $P$ and $Q$, as both $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $Q \Rightarrow P$, denoted $P \Longleftrightarrow Q$ or $P \leftrightarrow Q$, read as '$P$ if and only if $Q$', commonly abbreviated '$P$ iff $Q$'
12.1.1.11 Define the contrapositive of 'if $P$ then $Q$' as 'if not $Q$ then not $P$', denoted $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$
12.1.1.12 Recognise that an implication is equivalent to its contrapositive, that is $(P \Rightarrow Q) \Longleftrightarrow (\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P)$, and use this to prove results

### 12.1.3 Proof of inequalities
12.1.3.1 Prove results involving inequalities using the definition of $a > b$ for real $a$ and $b$, that is $a > b$ if and only if $a\ –\ b > 0$
12.1.3.2 Prove results involving inequalities using the property that squares of real numbers are non-negative, in particular $(a \pm b)^{2} \geq 0$
12.1.3.3 Prove and use results for numbers: if $a > b$ then $a \pm c > b \pm c$; if $a > b > 0$ then $\frac{1}{b} > \frac{1}{a} > 0 and vice versa; if $|a| > |b|$ then $a^{2} > b^{2}$ and vice versa; if $a > b$ and $b > c$ then $a > c$; if $a > b$ and $c > d$ then $a + c > b + d$; if $a > b$ and $c > 0$ then $ac > bc$; if $a > b$ and $c < 0$ then $ac < bc$
12.1.3.4 Prove and use the triangle inequality $|a + b| \leq |a| + |b|$ and interpret the inequality geometrically

12.1.3.9 Prove inequalities using graphical or calculus techniques or a combination of both

12.3.1.5 Refer to $a$ as 'the real part of the complex number $z = a + ib$', denoted by $Re(z)$

12.3.1.7 Classify numbers as belonging to the set of natural numbers $\mathbb{(}\mathbb{N}$), integers ($\mathbb{Z}$), rational numbers ($\mathbb{Q}$), real numbers ($\mathbb{R}$) or complex numbers ($\mathbb{C}$), each of which is an extension of the previous
12.3.1.8 Identify and use the condition for two complex numbers $z_{1}$ and $z_{2}$ to be equal, that is $z_{1} = z_{2}$ if and only if $Re\left( z_{1} \right) = Re\left( z_{2} \right)$ and $Im\left( z_{1} \right) = Im\left( z_{2} \right)$
